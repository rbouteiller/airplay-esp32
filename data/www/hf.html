Speaker configuration

No processing

The amplifier is running the part's built-in stereo program: plain left and right, digital volume only, no crossover, EQ or dynamics. Pick a configuration above to tune one, or leave it here if this part has no usable HybridFlow.

Tune in the order TI recommends: set the volume ceiling first, then the biquad EQ for the baseline response, then the compressor and smooth clipper for power limiting, then DBE low level, DBE high level, and PBE last. Each stage assumes the ones before it are settled.

The input feeds both ways in parallel. Each way has five biquads — the first is its crossover leg — and only the low way gets the bass enhancers. The high way's delay aligns its acoustic centre with the low way's. Tune the crossover first, then each way's EQ, then the compander and smooth clipper, and the bass enhancers last.

The first row of each way is its crossover leg; the four below it are free EQ. Nothing forces the two legs to share a corner or an alignment, but a complementary pair is what sums flat — the composite is what the listener hears, and it is the crossover region that decides whether the speaker sounds like one source or two.

The delay is a whole number of samples in steps of four, so at 44.1 kHz the choices are 0, 0.091, 0.181, 0.272 and 0.363 ms — about 31 mm of path length per step in air. It exists to line the tweeter up with the woofer whose acoustic centre sits further back; too much is as audible as none.

Only the low way feeds the bass enhancers, so PBE and DBE act on whatever this crossover leaves below the corner.

Fit to a measurement

Load a frequency response export — REW text, or any file with a frequency and a level in the first two columns — and the fitter places peaking filters to flatten it. Only the shape is fitted, never the level, and the two limits apply to the summed response rather than to any one filter. Setting max total boost to zero puts the whole correction at or below unity, which costs output level but consumes no headroom and cannot clip.

The fit range matters more than anything else here. A small speaker rolls off at both ends by more than the ±12 dB the data path has, so leaving the range wide spends filters fighting the roll-off and pulls the midrange down with it. Narrow it to the span the speaker can actually reproduce. Whatever the summed peak comes out at is how much you need to take off the output volume, and it is reported after each fit.

Ten biquads in series ahead of the bass enhancer, drawn as the design response before the headroom scaling a large boost needs. “All pass” here means unity, a section that does nothing; for a real all-pass choose Phase shift and read the phase graph. Alignment applies to the pass filters only, and Chebyshev reads the frequency as the edge of its ripple band, putting the pole about 23% past the number you type.

Tuning guide

Cuts before boosts — bands 1–5 to attenuate, 6–10 to lift. The sections run in series, so a boost early on can clip and every band after it then carries a copy of the clipped signal. The data path has +12 dB of headroom; keep individual peaks to 9–10 dB, and remember that two peaking bands close together simply add. Aim for the fewest bands that get the finished response inside ±3 to ±5 dB. A deep, narrow null that refuses to fill in when you boost it is a room reflection rather than the speaker — leave it alone.

Psychoacoustic Bass (PBE)

Response — extraction band vs effect shaping

Synthesises harmonics of the bass the speaker cannot reproduce. The corner sets the band that is analysed — drag the marker on the graph or type it — harmonic intensity sets how much synthetic content is generated, and effect intensity picks how sharply it is shaped. Harmonic intensity 0 disables the block. Both curves are driven entirely by the corner — every section in the extraction network is a fixed multiple of it — so the graph does not move when harmonic intensity changes, only the amount fed downstream does.

PBE only earns its keep at high output, so tune it last, after the compressor is settled, using material with real bass in it. Too much harmonic intensity turns into audible distortion. Whatever corner you land on, keep the DBE high-level high-pass at or below twice that frequency, or the bass enhancer removes the harmonics this block just synthesised. An occasional overflow at high output is normal; overflow that is constant, or that shows up at low output, means the path into this block is too hot and wants attenuating in the EQ.

The high-level pair is the protection curve: start with a fast-rolling high-pass at about 1.5× the driver's resonance, and if you need a steeper skirt add the second biquad an octave below that corner rather than raising the Q. The low-level pair is the bass lift. In a ported or passive-radiator box put the boost no lower than 0.85× the port tuning, ideally a little above it — further down and you are pumping the port rather than the cone.

Mixing thresholds

Mix ratio vs level — high-level EQ vs low-level EQ

Below the lower threshold only the low-level EQ is heard; above the upper threshold only the high-level EQ is. Between them the two are crossfaded. Lower runs −60 to −10 dBFS and upper −24 to 0, and they must stay at least 3 dB apart. Below about −18 dBFS a wider gap is needed, because the span is stored as a reciprocal: roughly 8 dB at −30 dBFS and 34 dB at −60.

The blend is not a straight ramp between the two markers. Both stored coefficients are amplitudes, not decibels, so it is linear in amplitude and bows on a dB axis — most of the travel happens in the top few dB of the span. It also sits below the dashed markers: the hardware holds both thresholds 4 dB down, so a −30 / −10 setting reaches the mixer as −34 and −14 dBFS.

There is no calculation that gives you the right pair — TI's guide says to find them by listening, on familiar material, at the volume the speaker will actually be used at.

Energy estimator

Sensing band

A band-pass listens to this range and averages its energy over the window to decide where the signal sits between the two thresholds. The curve is the real band-pass the firmware designs from the two boundaries (centre √(low·high), Q = centre / span), so its −3 dB points sit close to but not exactly on the values you enter.

Set the low boundary from whatever DC-blocking high-pass you put in the main EQ, and the high boundary at 1.1 to 1.25× the high-level high-pass corner — the estimator should hear the band the enhancer is working on and nothing above it. The window trades responsiveness for steadiness; TI's starting point is ten cycles of 100 Hz, which is the 100 ms this ships with.

Two split sections rather than four, and two gains: the mid band is whatever the other two leave behind, and the high band is always summed at unity. The flow ships the mid gain at −1, which is what makes the split recombine flat.

Band split
Low Mid High Composite

Four free biquads, not a fixed crossover: one section for the low band, two for the mid, one for the high. The defaults are a complementary Linkwitz-Riley pair, which is why they recombine flat, but nothing forces that — any alignment is allowed and the composite trace is how you judge it.

Linkwitz-Riley 2 is the recommended starting alignment because it is the one that sums to unity, and the two sections that bound a band must share a corner for that to hold — the mid's low-pass has to sit on the high band's high-pass, not near it. TI's suggested ranges are low up to about 300 Hz, mid from there to 5 kHz and high above, which is what the defaults use. To hear one band on its own, zero the other two mixer gains.

Each band trace is scaled by the magnitude of its mixer gain, so a band turned down visibly drops out. The sign cannot show on a magnitude plot — it only affects the composite, which is where a wrong polarity shows up as a notch at the corner.

Transfer curve

RegionModeRatioSlope
1:1 Threshold Curve

Region 1 is below threshold 1, region 2 sits between the two and region 3 is above threshold 2. Slope is the shown ratio inverted in compress mode, so a compress ratio under 1.0 in fact expands — watch the curve, not the label. Ratio 1.0 leaves a region untouched. Ratios are limited to 0.2–5.0. The curve is drawn through unity at full scale: this flow has no working per-region output offset, so a region's gain is set by its ratio alone.

Two shapes are worth knowing. A hard power limit stays at 1:1 up to the level the speaker is rated for and then goes straight to a very steep ratio; it protects well but pumps, and it needs a fast attack. A soft limit — what the defaults set up — stays at 1:1 until 0.85 to 0.9 of rated power, takes a mild ratio up to rated power, then clamps. TI asks for 25:1 or steeper on that last leg; this flow's coefficient stops at 5:1, so the smooth clipper is doing the rest of the work.

Because the curve is pinned at full scale, compressing the top does not pull the peaks down, it lifts everything underneath: the defaults come out about 1.3 dB louder below −2 dBFS. Where the curve is anchored in the DSP is not something the part will tell us, so treat that as the drawing's best guess. If you want a genuine power limit, take the same amount back out with master volume.

Detector timing (ms)

BandEnergyAttackDecay

Energy sets the level estimator's averaging window; attack and decay set how fast the gain follows it.

TI scales all three from the lowest frequency the band has to cope with. Taking t = 1/fmin, a fast detector is 5t energy, 2t attack and 10t decay; a slow one is 15t, 5t and 20t. The defaults are the fast set for fmin of 20 Hz, 300 Hz and 5 kHz. Fast costs a little distortion; slow sounds cleaner but lets more energy through, which is then the smooth clipper's problem.

Volume

Master volume drives the part's own volume registers and is the same one AirPlay sets, so it applies as you drag — it is not part of the Apply/Commit flow below, and the next volume change from a phone will overwrite it. Save volume also writes it to NVS so it survives a reboot.

The fine trim is a gain inside the flow, after the compander and before the smooth clipper, so it belongs to the tuning: it is applied as you drag, but only Commit writes it to SPIFFS. Range is ±0.25 dB; the coefficient itself could reach +6.02 dB before its 1/2 headroom pad saturates. Use it to match levels between units, not as a volume control.

This is the first thing to settle. Everything downstream — the compressor thresholds, the smooth clip point, where DBE and PBE start working — is judged against the loudest the system will ever be asked to play, so decide that before tuning anything else.

Output

The smooth clipper rounds the transfer curve off as it approaches the threshold instead of hard-limiting. 0 dB leaves full scale available. Coefficients are designed for the sample rate shown; the flow is rebuilt when it changes.

To set it the way TI does, drive the amplifier into a resistor matching the speaker's impedance, feed a sine at the highest input level you expect, and sweep the threshold down at the frequency your EQ boosts most until THD+N is where you want it — then look at the whole spectrum again, because lowering it too far trades peak headroom for a wideband noise floor. Once the clipper is active the part reports Overflow in its audio data status; that is the clipper working, not a fault.

Apply is audible immediately but is lost on reboot or when the flow is re-downloaded. Commit bakes the tuning into the flow binary in SPIFFS so it survives both.
